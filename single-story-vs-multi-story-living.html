One Story vs. Two: What Multi-Level Living Means As You Age

Guide · AgingInPlaceChecklist

The very first question on our checklist is how many stories the home has. It's first for a reason: it's the single factor that determines whether stairs are part of daily life at all, and it quietly changes how much every other answer matters. Two homes with identical hazards score differently if one of them requires climbing stairs to reach a bedroom and the other doesn't.

Why stairs carry so much weight

Stairs combine several risk factors into one spot: they require balance and leg strength that can change gradually enough to go unnoticed, they're often the dimmest-lit part of a home, and a fall on stairs tends to be more serious than a fall on a flat floor. It's not just about whether someone can climb stairs today — it's about whether the home still works if that changes next year, or after a minor injury or surgery makes stairs temporarily harder.

That's also why our checklist asks separately whether there's a full bathroom and a bedroom on the main floor. A two-story home where daily life — sleeping, bathing — never actually requires the stairs is a very different situation from one where climbing them is unavoidable every day.

When multi-story living is genuinely fine

Not every multi-story home is a risk. If stairs have handrails on both sides, are well-lit, aren't cluttered, and nobody in the home has a fall history or a mobility aid, the stairs themselves may simply be a non-issue — that's exactly why the checklist weighs stairs alongside those other factors rather than scoring "two stories" as an automatic red flag on its own.

If moving to single-story living isn't realistic

Moving is expensive, disruptive, and often not what people actually want to do — staying in a familiar home is usually the whole point of aging in place. If that's the situation, a few options come up often in practice:

  • Converting a main-floor room into a bedroom (a den or dining room, for instance), paired with adding or upgrading a main-floor bathroom, so day-to-day life doesn't require stairs even if the upper floor still exists.
  • A stair lift for homes where a full main-floor conversion isn't possible, keeping upper floors accessible without requiring the stairs to be climbed unassisted.
  • Restructuring which rooms get used daily — deliberately shifting laundry, a home office, or other regular activities to the main floor to reduce how often stairs are actually used, even before any construction happens.

Which of these makes sense depends heavily on the specific floor plan, budget, and how much of daily life can realistically move downstairs — the kind of thing a CAPS specialist can assess in person and price out.
