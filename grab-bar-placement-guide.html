Grab Bars 101: Where They Actually Belong

Guide · AgingInPlaceChecklist

"Does the bathroom have grab bars near the toilet and shower or tub?" is one of the highest-weighted questions on our home safety checklist, and for good reason — the bathroom is where most in-home falls happen, and grab bars are one of the cheapest, highest-impact fixes available. But a grab bar only helps if it's in the right place, at the right height, and anchored into something that can actually hold body weight.

Near the toilet

The most useful setup is a horizontal or angled bar mounted on the wall beside the toilet, roughly 33 to 36 inches above the floor, positioned so it's within easy reach while sitting down or standing up. Some layouts also use a fold-down bar on the open side when there isn't a wall on both sides. The goal is something to push against on the way down and pull against on the way up — not a bar that's decorative or just out of reach.

In the shower or tub

A shower or tub needs more than one bar to be genuinely useful:

  • A vertical or angled bar near the entry, to hold onto while stepping in or out.
  • A horizontal bar along the back or side wall, at a similar 33–36 inch height, for stability while standing or sitting on a shower seat.
  • Non-slip strips or a mat on the shower/tub floor — a grab bar doesn't help much if the feet go out first.

Our checklist separates "grab bars near both" from "only one" from "neither" because that gap matters. One bar near the toilet and none in the shower still leaves the highest-risk moment — stepping over a tub wall — completely unaddressed.

Mistakes that make a bar useless

Anchored into drywall only. A grab bar has to hold a person's full weight, suddenly, off-balance. It needs to be screwed into wall studs or blocking installed specifically for this purpose — a standard drywall anchor will pull straight out under real load.
Confused with a towel bar. Towel bars look similar but aren't rated to bear weight. If it came from the towel aisle, it's not a grab bar, no matter where it's mounted.
Wrong height or angle. A bar mounted too high, too low, or at an angle that doesn't match how someone actually moves through the space (sitting to standing, stepping over a tub wall) won't get used, even if it's technically installed correctly.

When to bring in a specialist

Finding wall studs in the right spot, choosing bar length and angle for the specific fixture layout, and meeting code height requirements is exactly what a Certified Aging-in-Place Specialist (CAPS) is trained to get right the first time — worth it for something that has to hold up in an emergency.
